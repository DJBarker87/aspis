\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Succinct transparent arguments reduce a statement about a computation to a
small number of algebraic and authenticated-opening checks.  A deployed
private-spend verifier adds another set of obligations.  The proof must bind
the exact public statement, the verifier must authenticate every value it
uses, the nullifier must control a real state transition, and the
implementation must preserve the order assumed by the Fiat--Shamir argument.
Each part is simple to describe in isolation.  Their composition is not.

\Aspis{} brings that composition into one auditable development.  It has three
different kinds of evidence:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Lean definitions and proofs for the mathematical model;
  \item focused Rust-to-Lean translations and source-shaped bridge theorems;
  \item build and transaction records for the binary that ran on Solana.
\end{enumerate}
Together they provide a checked mathematical verifier, a selected source
connection from one successful translated callback through the complete
relation caller, the compiled program, and one finalized execution.  The
report keeps the remaining hash-callback, compiler, cryptographic, and runtime
boundaries separate from the statements that Lean checks.

The principal result is a checked security decomposition, not an unconditional
claim of 100-bit deployed security.  Every accepted false outcome in the
modeled experiment lies in one ideal-protocol event or one of the explicitly
named primitive, credential, and runtime events.  Lean proves the event
decomposition and derives the combined probability bound in one sample space.
For the selected verifier path, separate theorems obtain the deterministic
transcript, opening, FRI, claim-table, relation, and accumulator evidence from
one successful translated Rust execution instead of accepting those
intermediate equalities as inputs.  The final theorem uses the same four-round
execution for the decoded relation values and both accumulators, proves the
twelve-component and compact final dots, and reaches the maintained
security-event conclusion.

The deterministic selected-call theorem and the work-normalized probability
experiment remain separate results.  A further experiment-level theorem must
place accepted calls in one causal probability law, map the six-way
classification into the probability experiment's events, and discharge the
published and primitive bounds before the conditional (2^{-100}) endpoint
applies to the selected implementation.
The translated entry path checks consistency of the live statement and its
digest.  A separate theorem must still identify those Rust statement fields
with the abstract public statement used by the mathematical false-acceptance
and theft experiments.

The mathematical background is kept short.  Most of the report is devoted to
the formal model, security experiment, proof reductions, and source locations.
Lean declarations are cited by stable names instead of line numbers, which
change during maintenance.

\subsection{Contributions}

The contributions reported here are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A machine-checked decomposition of accepted false outcomes into one
        ideal-protocol event and eighteen named external events.  The old and
        new ledgers have a proved union equality, and the resulting
        conditional budget theorem uses one probability space without an
        independence assumption.
  \item A Lean proof of the released mathematical verifier: the complete
        one-input/one-output spend relation after extraction, exact M31 circle
        domains and encoders, four coefficient and verifier-weight folds, a
        prefix-timed four-round candidate argument, and the exact bounded
        distinct-query calculation.
  \item Byte-level transcript and five-tree Merkle specifications, together
        with focused Charon/Aeneas translations and Lean proofs connecting
        one successful translated production verifier run to the transcript,
        authenticated values, FRI checks, exact claim table, initial relation
        value and decoded relation tail used by the mathematical argument.
        The same accepted execution supplies the exact main and compact
        accumulator states and the complete relation caller used by the final
        selected-path theorem.
  \item Fixed-victim and observed-history theft reductions, plus explicit
        state models for account checks, the non-overwritable spent marker,
        rollback, finalized persistence, and close/refund behavior.
  \item A reproducible source-to-binary record and archived mainnet execution
        evidence binding one program, proof, public statement, transaction,
        and before/after state.
\end{itemize}

To our knowledge, this is the first finalized Solana mainnet transaction in
which a Solana L1 program directly verifies a trusted-setup-free proof of a
note-based private-spend relation and, upon acceptance, atomically records the
spent nullifier and advances the committed note state.  This is a claim about
that specific combination, not a claim that Aspis is the first private-payment
system, shielded pool, or transparent verifier on Solana.
\Cref{sec:related-work} gives the comparison and its search date;
\cref{sec:evaluation} identifies the transaction, program, proof, public
statement, and before/after state.

\subsection{Scope}

The \(2^{-100}\) result is a conditional budget theorem for the
work-normalized experiment defined in Lean.  It is not the headline claim
that deployed V5 has an unconditional 100-bit false-acceptance bound.  The
selected accepting verifier path is connected to the maintained models in
Lean through its complete relation caller.  The theorem works for any terminal
evaluator because later security reasoning does not consume its value.  This
does not verify that evaluator, the compiler, every program function, or the
Solana runtime.  The published decoding and BCS Fiat--Shamir results, SHA-256
and Poseidon2 security, the proof assistant and translation tools, compiler,
and Solana runtime are stated as trusted inputs.
The mathematical privacy and theft reductions are reported separately from a
full production privacy or raw theft bound.  \Cref{sec:limitations} gives the
complete list.
